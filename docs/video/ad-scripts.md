# Advertising scripts — patient and clinician

Two films, shot from one production. Written to be handed to a videographer as
they are: every beat carries its timing, what is on screen, and what is said.

**Runtime target: 75–90 seconds each.** Not three minutes. An ad that needs
three minutes has not decided what it is about. Both scripts below are timed to
90 and can be cut to 60 by dropping the beats marked *(trim first)*.

**Read the claims guardrails at the end before writing a single new line.** This
is a health product; some sentences that sound like ordinary marketing are
regulatory problems.

# Film 1 — Patient

**The one idea:** you are the only person who attends every one of your
appointments — and you are the only one without the record.

Everything else in the film is proof of that sentence. If a beat does not
serve it, cut the beat.

**Tone:** warm, unhurried, adult. Not a startup explainer. Closer to a bank or
an insurer that respects you than to a wellness app. No ukulele. No whooshes.

---

### 0:00 — 0:14 · The problem, felt rather than explained

**Visual.** A woman in her fifties in a waiting room. She is going through a
plastic wallet of papers — a folded letter, a pharmacy printout, a photo of a
prescription on her phone. She finds one, loses another. Handheld, close, no
dialogue. Cut to her being asked something across a desk and pausing a beat too
long before answering.

**On screen** (appears at 0:09, small, lower third):
> Every appointment. Same questions.

**VO** *(begins 0:04, calm)*
> You have been to a dozen appointments. Different clinics, different systems,
> different people. Every one of them holds a piece of your health.
>
> You are the only person who was at all of them. And you are the only one
> without the record.

*(48 words)*

---

### 0:14 — 0:24 · The turn

**Visual.** Hard cut to black for a quarter-second. Then the OneCare record
assembling on a phone — not a UI tour, a single continuous scroll where
medicines, readings, and documents come together in one place. Real motion, no
fake data flying in.

**On screen:**
> One record. Yours.

**VO**
> OneCare gives you one record you actually hold. Everything in one place, and
> everything with a source — where it came from, and who put it there.

*(28 words)*

---

### 0:24 — 0:44 · Proof one: sharing, and taking it back

**Visual.** Thumb selects categories on the share screen — medicines, readings,
documents — then a clinician's view opening on a tablet showing exactly those
and nothing else. Then, deliberately, the same thumb revoking. The tablet view
empties.

**On screen** (two cards, timed to the action):
> You choose what they see.
> You can take it back.

**VO**
> Share what you want, with who you want, for as long as you want. Choose the
> parts — your medicines, your readings, your documents. And when you end it, it
> ends. Not tomorrow. Then.

*(35 words)*

> **Note to editor.** The revoke beat is the most important two seconds in the
> film. Hold on the emptying screen a fraction longer than feels comfortable.

---

### 0:44 — 1:02 · Proof two: nothing changes without you

**Visual.** A notification on her phone. She opens it: her doctor has suggested
a change to a dose, with a short reason in the doctor's own words. Two buttons.
She reads. She presses **Accept**. The medicine updates.

**On screen:**
> Your doctor can suggest. Only you can accept.

**VO**
> Your clinician can suggest a change — a different dose, a new medicine, or
> stopping one — and tell you why. Nothing changes in your record until you say
> yes. And if you say no, that is recorded too.

*(41 words)*

> **This is the beat the film exists for.** It is the whole product in one
> gesture. Give it room; do not cut away early.

---

### 1:02 — 1:16 · Proof three: an assistant that admits what it doesn't know *(trim first)*

**Visual.** She photographs a pill packet. The medicine fills itself in. She
types a question — *"can I take this with my blood pressure tablets?"* — and the
answer appears, citing the label. Then a second, deliberately unglamorous shot:
the assistant saying it could not check something.

**On screen:**
> Answers from the label. Not from guesswork.

**VO**
> Ask about your medicines and get an answer from the manufacturer's own label.
> And when it cannot check something, it says so — because the useful answer and
> the confident one are not always the same.

*(37 words)*

---

### 1:16 — 1:28 · The payoff

**Visual.** Return to the waiting room. Same woman, same chair — the plastic
wallet is gone. She is asked the question. This time she just answers, and turns
her phone around.

**VO**
> Your health happened to you. The record should belong to you too.

*(13 words)*

---

### 1:28 — 1:35 · End card

**On screen:**
> **OneCare**
> Your health record. Yours.
> onecare.you

**VO** — none. Let it land in silence.

# Film 2 — Clinician

**The one idea:** you get ten minutes and half the story, and the risk of what
you are not told is yours to carry.

**Tone:** peer to peer. Brisk, unsentimental, respectful of their time. No
stethoscope-on-a-desk stock footage. No actor pretending to be moved.

---

### 0:00 — 0:12 · The problem

**Visual.** A clinician between patients. Three windows open. A patient sits
down and says the line every clinician has heard: *"I'm on something for my
blood pressure — I don't remember the name."* Hold on the clinician's face.

**On screen:**
> Ten minutes. Half the history.

**VO**
> You have ten minutes, and whatever they can remember. Then you carry the risk
> of everything they could not.

*(21 words)*

---

### 0:12 — 0:26 · The turn

**Visual.** The chart opening: medicines with where each came from, readings
with a trend, documents, and — held for a moment — the ones the patient has
stopped taking, marked as such with their reason.

**On screen:**
> They arrive with the record. Not the memory.

**VO**
> With OneCare, they arrive with their record. What they take, what they have
> stopped taking and why, what their last readings were, and where every piece
> of it came from.

*(31 words)*

> **Note.** The stopped-medication line is the strongest single claim in this
> film for a working clinician. Make sure the marker is legible on screen.

---

### 0:26 — 0:44 · Proof one: the note writes itself

**Visual.** The clinician finishes with a patient and speaks a short dictation.
Cut to the structured note already filed against the encounter. No typing.

**On screen:**
> Dictate. It files itself.

**VO**
> Dictate at the end of a consultation and it lands in the record, in the right
> place, attributed to you. The admin that used to follow you home stays at
> work.

*(32 words)*

---

### 0:44 — 1:04 · Proof two: suggest, and it is agreed

**Visual.** Clinician opens the patient's medicine, taps **Suggest a change**,
types a new dose and a one-line reason, sends. Cut to the patient's phone
accepting. Cut back: the clinician's list now shows it accepted.

**On screen:**
> You suggest. They accept. Both halves recorded.

**VO**
> Suggest a change to a medicine and say why. It goes to the patient to accept —
> and when they do, their record updates and so does yours. If they decline, you
> see that, and you see their reason.

*(41 words)*

> **The pitch inside the pitch:** this is adherence information no system
> normally gives them. A declined suggestion is a conversation they would
> otherwise have had six months late.

---

### 1:04 — 1:18 · Proof three: the mistake you can take back *(trim first)*

**Visual.** A document sent, then a beat of realisation, then the withdraw
action. Cut to the patient's side: the file is gone, and in its place a clear
note saying it was withdrawn and why.

**On screen:**
> Sent it to the wrong person? Take it back.

**VO**
> Send a letter to the wrong patient and you can withdraw it — properly, from
> everywhere it reached, with a record of what happened. Every practice has had
> that afternoon. Now it has an answer.

*(35 words)*

---

### 1:18 — 1:30 · The payoff and end card

**Visual.** The clinician closing the chart and looking up as the next patient
comes in. Unhurried.

**VO**
> Better information. Less admin. And a patient who knows exactly what you can
> see.

*(14 words)*

**On screen:**
> **OneCare**
> The record, before they sit down.
> onecare.you/clinicians

# Shared production notes

**Casting.** Both leads should look like the actual users. The patient film
should not star a 28-year-old; the person who most needs this is 45–70 and
managing more than one condition. The clinician should read as somebody eight
years into practice, not a professor.

**Diversity is not decoration here.** OneCare is used across several countries.
Cast accordingly, and make sure the medicines shown are ones that exist under
the names shown in the target market.

**Pace.** Cut on action, not on beats of music. The VO is the spine; picture
serves it.

**Music.** One piece, sparse, piano or muted strings, no build to a drop. It
should be almost unnoticed until the end card, where it should stop rather than
fade.

**Screen capture must be real.** No motion-graphics mock-ups of the app. Record
the actual product with seeded demo data. A fake UI is spotted instantly by
exactly the people we are trying to convince.

**Text on screen must be readable on a phone at arm's length.** Assume two
thirds of views are muted and vertical.

**Deliverables.** 16:9 master, 9:16 vertical cut, 1:1 square. Subtitles burned
in on the vertical and square cuts.

# Screen recordings to capture

Everything below is real product. Capture at 2× device resolution, 60fps, with
seeded demo data only.

**Patient film**
1. The record assembling — one continuous scroll: medicines, readings, documents
2. Share screen: selecting categories, then the clinician's view showing only
   those
3. **Revoking a share, and the clinician's view emptying** — the hero shot
4. A suggested medication change arriving, being read, and being accepted
5. Scanning a pill packet and the fields filling in
6. Asking an interaction question and getting a label-cited answer
7. The assistant saying it could not check something — capture this properly, it
   is a feature and not an error state

**Clinician film**
1. Opening a patient chart with medicines, provenance and trends visible
2. A medication marked as stopped by the patient, with their reason
3. Dictation → the filed note against the encounter
4. Suggest a change: the dialogue, sending, and the accepted state returning
5. Withdrawing a document, and the patient-side remnant that replaces it
6. The risk badge being opened to show the reasons behind it

# Claims guardrails — read before writing

This is a health platform. Some ordinary advertising sentences create
regulatory exposure. These are not stylistic preferences.

**Never say or imply:**

- That OneCare **diagnoses, treats, prevents or monitors disease.** That is the
  definition of a medical device under EU MDR and the FDA's software guidance.
  OneCare records, organises and shares information. It does not assess.
- That using it **improves outcomes, saves lives, prevents hospital admissions
  or reduces errors** — any outcome claim needs evidence we do not have.
- That the assistant gives **medical advice**, or any phrasing like "ask our AI
  what's wrong with you". It answers questions about medicines from published
  labelling and reference sources, and it says when it cannot.
- That the record is **complete**. It contains what has been added to it.
- **"Secure"** as a bare adjective, or any specific unqualified security or
  compliance claim. Describe the behaviour instead: you choose what is shared,
  and ending a share takes effect immediately.
- Anything comparing OneCare to a **named competitor or EHR**.
- That a clinician **can change** a patient's record. They suggest; the patient
  accepts. Getting this backwards in an ad contradicts the product and the
  documentation.

**Do say, because it is true and checkable:**

- One record the patient holds, with the source of every item.
- The patient chooses what is shared and can end it at any time, effective
  immediately.
- A clinician suggests changes; nothing is written until the patient accepts;
  a decline is recorded.
- Medicine answers cite the manufacturer's label, and the assistant says when it
  could not check.
- A document sent in error can be withdrawn, and the patient is told.

**Required on-screen, minimum two seconds, legible:**

> OneCare does not provide medical advice. Always speak to a qualified
> healthcare professional about your care.

**Do not show real patient data, real clinician names, or a real practice's
branding** without written permission from that practice. Seeded demo data
only.

**Final copy goes past counsel** before the film is cut, not after. Changing a
line in a script costs nothing; re-recording a voiceover does.

# Cutdowns

**60 seconds.** Drop the beats marked *(trim first)* — proof three in each film.
The remaining structure holds without a re-edit of the VO.

**30 seconds, patient.** Waiting room (0:00–0:10) → revoke shot (0:10–0:20) →
accept-a-change (0:20–0:27) → end card. VO: the first two sentences and the
last one.

**30 seconds, clinician.** "I don't remember the name" (0:00–0:08) → the chart
with provenance (0:08–0:18) → suggest-and-accept (0:18–0:27) → end card.

**6 seconds, bumper.** The revoke shot, silent, with one line:
> You can take it back.
